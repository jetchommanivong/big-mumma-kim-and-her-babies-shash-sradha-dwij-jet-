# Interview P7

**Research project:** Why We Photograph: Meaning and Motivation Behind the Everyday Photo

---

**Interviewer:** Do you consent to being recorded?

**Participant:** I consent.

**Interviewer:** Thanks so much for taking the time to talk with me today. The project we're exploring focuses on how people relate to photography — including the photos we take, the photos taken of us, and what they mean to us. This is a casual conversation, and there are no right or wrong answers. Feel free to take your time, tell stories, and go on tangents. If you don't want to answer a particular question, we can move on.

Tell me about the last time you had your photo taken. What was the occasion, or who took it? How did you feel about it?

**Participant:** I don't even remember the last time I had my photo taken.

**Interviewer:** Really?

**Participant:** It was probably me, because I took a family photo, and then took a photo of myself to photoshop into the family photo.

**Interviewer:** You didn't take a selfie?

**Participant:** I like doing it that way, because I like making him look intentionally bad, with me just somewhere in the photo.

**Interviewer:** So a little bit of fun?

**Participant:** Yeah.

**Interviewer:** What's your favourite photo you've taken this year?

**Participant:** It was probably late last year, but it's a photo I took on film in Nepal, of a guy in his Kathmandu shop, just tinkering away at whatever it was he was working on. Black and white film.

**Interviewer:** What's the story behind that photo? Did you travel there specifically?

**Participant:** My partner's Nepalese, and every time I go and visit, I take my cameras and shoot. Kathmandu has become probably my favourite place to shoot — just the life, the stuff you see and can capture. It's got a lot of life, a lot to see. This gentleman was solely focused on what he was doing, amongst the craziness of his shop. That's what he'd set up, and I took the shot and kept going.

**Interviewer:** Sounds like a beautiful place.

**Participant:** It is an amazing place.

**Interviewer:** When you're out and about, what makes something feel worth photographing to you?

**Participant:** I think for me it's more instinctual. I'm not really looking for composition, or certain colours, or certain subjects — it's just a feeling, and that's the way I shoot, I guess. Something that grabs my attention is probably how I shoot. There's merit to being more deliberate — choosing a subject, or a colour palette, before you go out — that definitely leads to a clearer body of work. But for me personally, right now, it's very instinctual, very intuitive — whatever I see.

**Interviewer:** Have you ever gone to a place purposefully just to take a photo of a particular area?

**Participant:** I have — very few and far between, though, because a lot of my travel at the moment is Nepal. I've got a lot of spots I want to hit when I go there. I can't always spend a lot of time shooting because I've got to be with my partner too. But I do have a list of places — even here, like when I was back in Melbourne, there were a few spots I knew I wanted to shoot.

**Interviewer:** Tell me what you usually do with the photos you've taken. Do you share them, keep them on a hard drive, or print them out?

**Participant:** I did keep them on a hard drive. Sometimes I post them, but very rarely, because I'm terrible at social media. Recently I've been getting into printing more, framing, and displaying my work at home — or giving one to a friend if they want it. Normally they live on a hard drive, but I dropped that hard drive, with eight years' worth of photography on it.

**Interviewer:** Oh no.

**Participant:** I've lost basically all my bodies of work. So — start again.

**Interviewer:** If someone scrolled through your camera roll, what impression would they get? What do you think it says about you?

**Participant:** My camera roll doesn't really have a lot of my photography in it. Honestly, it's anything — doesn't have to be photography — screenshots of memes, that kind of thing. A lot of it's just stupid photos of me and my partner, or me and my mates. Probably unserious and funny. I think the few bits of my work in there are a contrast to a lot of the mess you'd probably see.

**Interviewer:** Do you care about the photos you take? Why or why not?

**Participant:** I do care. I think if you're in this space, it's not logical not to care about the body of work you're producing — that's just my opinion. But if you're creating something, even if it's not good, or not what you wanted it to be — that's subjective — it still has merit, it's still a step. So I believe you should care, and I do. I think calm is very important.

**Interviewer:** Last question — tell me about a time someone shared a photo of you without asking. Maybe your partner posted a photo of the two of you, but you didn't know she was going to post that particular one?

**Participant:** I don't even know — she's definitely posted photos without asking me, but I'm married to her, so she can do whatever she wants, really. My group of friends don't really post anything either, so it's not like someone's taken something I didn't want out there and posted it. No, I haven't really had experience with that, I don't think.

**Interviewer:** That's everything I wanted to ask. Before we finish, is there anything you'd like to talk about further — anything about photography, taking photos, or being photographed?

**Participant:** To be a good photographer, you probably should be comfortable in front of the camera, not just behind it.

**Interviewer:** Yeah, right.

**Participant:** Which is something I'm not overly enjoying, but it's probably a good thing to learn — to be comfortable in front of the camera, especially if you're shooting portraits and you expect other people to be in front of it. That's one thing I've learned while working in this space.

**Interviewer:** That's pretty good advice. Thank you so much for sharing your experiences.
