# Interview P5

**Research project:** Why We Photograph: Meaning and Motivation Behind the Everyday Photo

---

**Interviewer:** Tell me about the last time you had your photo taken — a photo taken by someone else.

**Participant:** A few weeks ago, at dinner with a friend from uni, at a Thai restaurant. My friend wanted to try different flash techniques, take photos in different ways — he's a photographer, wanted to practice using the camera and try to get better photos. He took photos of us one by one.

**Interviewer:** Was there a particular occasion?

**Participant:** The dinner was just for catching up, but the photos were just for fun.

**Interviewer:** How did you feel about the experience?

**Participant:** It was quite fun — you see some weird photos, some pretty photos, different ways of taking photos and showing them differently.

**Interviewer:** What's been your favourite photo you've taken this year?

**Participant:** The trade show day for our project. That kind of moment is the final moment for the whole cause — everyone is so enjoying it, doesn't matter if I'm in the photo or someone else is, everyone is enjoying the project, the event. It was my favourite this year.

**Interviewer:** When you're out and about, what makes something feel worth photographing to you?

**Participant:** Every time I try to keep a good memory, a happy moment. For example, I took a lot of photos when my children grew up, so I can go back to that time — I saw my daughter's baby photos and felt like I really miss that moment, and I can't return to it. It's worth more than other things because she was so cute and little, and I can't go back to that.

**Interviewer:** Can you think of a recent example?

**Participant:** My daughter turned ten this year, first of August — a big milestone, two-digit number — so I took a lot of photos and videos during her birthday party.

**Interviewer:** What do you usually do with the photos you take?

**Participant:** If I have time, I'll post it on Instagram stories, or sometimes Facebook too. My parents are in Taiwan, so it's a way for us to communicate — they can see my kids are here, that we're all happy in Australia. I'd just post it online.

**Interviewer:** So mostly with friends and family?

**Participant:** Yes.

**Interviewer:** What makes you decide to share something?

**Participant:** Mainly to share with family and friends back in Taiwan, because it's really hard to keep the connection over that long distance — it's a wonderful way to share that. Another reason is to keep memories for my life. If I take photos but don't organise or post anything, it'll just stay in my phone and I won't check it again. If I post something, sometimes Facebook reminds me — okay, last year I had this event — and brings the memories back.

**Interviewer:** So it's also about keeping track of what you've done?

**Participant:** Yeah.

**Interviewer:** If somebody scrolled through your camera roll, what impression would they get?

**Participant:** A lot of my kids' photos, and also my pets. Probably hard to find photos that aren't of my kids or pets.

**Interviewer:** What do you think that says about you as a person?

**Participant:** That I'm a mum, I love my children, my pets, I'm always busy with them, and I bring them to travel everywhere.

**Interviewer:** Do you think your camera roll reflects who you are as a person?

**Participant:** Yes, definitely.

**Interviewer:** When you take pictures, do you care about them afterwards?

**Participant:** It depends on my age. When I was younger — high school, university age — I cared more about photos, tried to organise them, print or share them. After a certain point I feel, okay, I took some photos, I know I have them, but I don't pay much attention. I think it's because there are other more important things to focus on, so probably less attention on photos and little things.

**Interviewer:** Do some photos matter more to you than others?

**Participant:** Definitely — different photos have different weight, depending on the scenario, the moment, who was taking or being photographed. On iPhone you can heart a photo to mark it special — I think that's important to me. For example, my grandfather's photos, my kids as babies, my family's big photos — I don't want to waste time scrolling through everything, so those are the ones that matter most, even if I hadn't thought about it that way before.

**Interviewer:** Can you tell me about a time someone shared a photo of you without asking?

**Participant:** About ten, thirteen years ago, when I was a backpacker in Australia. I worked for a contractor, supervising, and one of the workers wasn't satisfied with the job. She thought I managed things, so she posted my photo on Facebook, along with a friend's, saying we were "dodgy" and other things — most of it was totally untrue.

**Interviewer:** How did you feel about it?

**Participant:** Uncomfortable — because it's posted publicly, and if people search the name they could find it. Definitely uncomfortable, and upset.

**Interviewer:** Did you ask her to take it down?

**Participant:** Yes, but she ignored us. I posted something in response, though I didn't talk to her directly — my boss did.

**Interviewer:** Looking back, would you change anything?

**Participant:** Not really — after my boss talked to her, it was okay. People who saw both posts felt mine was more reasonable and logical, while hers was untrue, so I felt okay about how it resolved.

**Interviewer:** Is there anything about photography or being photographed we haven't covered that feels important?

**Participant:** Now that I'm a mum, I feel asking permission before taking or posting a photo is quite important, especially with kids — you should ask the parents. In Taiwan, not many people ask, but here in Australia, most of my friends ask before posting, even though they know I'm okay with it — they still ask.
