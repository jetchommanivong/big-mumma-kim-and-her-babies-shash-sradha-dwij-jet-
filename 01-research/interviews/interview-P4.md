# Interview P4

**Research project:** Why We Photograph: Meaning and Motivation Behind the Everyday Photo

---

**Interviewer:** Tell me about the last time you had your photo taken.

**Participant:** The most recent one would be when I went to Japan. My sister was supposed to click a few pictures of mine, since we were doing a task where we'd each photograph the other.

**Interviewer:** How did you feel about it?

**Participant:** It was more of a task, actually — explaining to her how to click certain pictures, because she isn't really good at it. I had to explain how to hold it, which direction to focus. She usually took pictures that weren't that good. The overall experience was a mixed bag of emotion — sometimes I got angry, and eventually I just gave up.

**Interviewer:** Are you good at photography?

**Participant:** Not really, but I did a bachelor's in media and graphics, so there was a subject on how to record certain videos — grid lines, capturing images under specific rules. I still follow that grid pattern, focusing on the corners and the subject on those lines.

**Interviewer:** What's your favourite photo you've taken this year?

**Participant:** By me, from my recent trip — one from Nara Deer Park, of a male deer with unique, shiny horns. When I click pictures, I don't want to capture people in the shot — I'll wait at a spot until there's no one there before taking it. If it's about people, I'm not always looking for their expression; sometimes I just want to capture the essence of the location itself. There was a popular bamboo forest with a lot of people, so I waited until the zone was empty. There are tools to erase people afterwards, but it degrades the quality — even good software doesn't do it well, at least on iPhone. On the consent side, I don't want to capture faces unless I'm out of options, because I'm saving these as long-term memories and don't want other people's faces holding those memories.

**Interviewer:** Is that because you want to keep the photo as a memory, or because of the emotion of the place?

**Participant:** A mix of both. My family was really into travelling, and back then we only had basic digital cameras — everything was on one SD card, which eventually got destroyed, so all those memories were erased. I've learned about myself that I won't remember the name of a place unless I have a picture to go back and check. My memory is like a fish. My sister has a very good memory and can track where we've been without pictures — I can't.

**Interviewer:** When you're out and about, what makes something feel worth photographing?

**Participant:** Something natural that pops up unexpectedly. When I lived in Dresden I saw multiple rainbows and tried to capture them each time — once I saw two at the same time. It gave me a good sense of seeing different colours.

**Interviewer:** Is it about the view, or the moment itself?

**Participant:** I think it's because I'm into art, painting — when I see different shades, I try to imagine them differently. It doesn't have to be fancy — it can just be a normal thing, like sitting by a lake and seeing concentric ripples. Even emotions — if my dogs are happy doing something, I'll record it so it becomes a good memory, because not everything is permanent. I look back at those pictures or videos when I miss home.

**Interviewer:** What do you usually do with the photos you take? Do you share them?

**Participant:** Either I'll post it on Instagram if it's a personal image — like my landlord's cats sitting on me — I'll share that with my mum. It can be a larger audience, or just family, or a close one I'm sharing memories with.

**Interviewer:** What makes you decide to share something?

**Participant:** It depends on the picture. Recently I got a new phone in a blue shade, and noticed my tablet, laptop and phone were all the same colour, so I took a picture — but didn't post it, because I worried people would think I was showing off. Different images are meant for different people.

**Interviewer:** So you also think about others' perspectives when sharing?

**Participant:** I check my story after posting, to see if it looks good from a viewer's perspective rather than the poster's. It's not about perfection, more about whether it makes sense to me. I used to think about what others thought more; now it's more about whether the colours are good or if there's a grammatical error in the caption.

**Interviewer:** If there was a space where people shared photos collectively, would you want to stay anonymous or be credited?

**Participant:** If I put real effort into capturing something — like a photo I took of the Sphinx with a pyramid and unusually glowing clouds behind it, one I still love — I wouldn't mind having my name on it. It's an effort someone makes to capture the moment. I don't mind either way, honestly.

**Interviewer:** Have you ever wanted to just be in a moment rather than photograph it?

**Participant:** Yes — there was a specific spot, an old, quiet, ancient civilisation lake, where my mum and I just sat and listened to the birds instead of taking pictures. If I've already taken enough pictures of a place before, I'll choose to just experience it rather than capture it again.

**Interviewer:** If someone scrolled through your camera roll, what impression would they get?

**Participant:** Recently, mostly screenshots of research material for different projects — they'd probably think I'm a nerd. If they scroll further, multiple pictures of me, because my sister shoots in burst mode, knowing I'll complain about most of them — I just pick a favourite and leave the rest until I run out of storage.

**Interviewer:** What do you think your camera roll says about you as a person?

**Participant:** It would say less about me, more about the environment or surroundings.

**Interviewer:** Does it reflect who you are?

**Participant:** In a way, yes — because unless I'm extremely close to someone, the only pictures in my phone are of close family. Even people I call friends, I'll send them the picture and then delete it from my own gallery. It's not about being self-absorbed — it's that I only keep memories I consider worthy, based on what value that person holds in my life.

**Interviewer:** Do you care about the photos you take? Do some matter more than others?

**Participant:** Not really — my recent pictures are mostly of cats or birds, and I don't care much about those. I care about the pictures I've taken in the past that I can't go back and retake — like my old dog Shiro, who loved wearing a birthday hat and sitting in front of a cake for photos. Since he passed, we haven't celebrated any of our other dogs' birthdays the same way. Those are memories I cherish.

**Interviewer:** Tell me about a time someone shared a photo of you without asking.

**Participant:** My mum shared a picture of me with my aunt without telling me. I found out when I visited and my aunt mentioned she'd already seen it. I got angry, not because our relationship is bad, but because it's not really about permission — she can do what she wants since the picture is on her phone. My aunt tends to be judgy, pointing out small things, so I keep some distance because of that. Otherwise, I haven't really experienced anyone sharing my photo without asking.

**Interviewer:** If that happened again, would you handle it differently?

**Participant:** I'd share pictures using a one-time-view option, so they can't be saved and re-shared. It's more like: I'm sharing it with you, you see it, and it's done — no option to pass it on.

**Interviewer:** Is there anything else you'd like to add?

**Participant:** Something I've noticed is the gradual degradation of pictures over time — even saved ones become less clear, unlike newer cameras with better clarity. I wish that wasn't the case, that a picture could stay as clear as when it was taken, and stay with you the way you originally saw it.
