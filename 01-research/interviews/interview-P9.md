# Interview P9

**Research project:** Why We Photograph: Meaning and Motivation Behind the Everyday Photo

---

**Interviewer:** Can you please tell me about the last time you had your photo taken?

**Participant:** The last time would be last week, when I did a road trip near Birmingham. It was an hour away, and I wanted to check out a local castle I hadn't been to that everyone told me was interesting.

**Interviewer:** Who took it?

**Participant:** My partner did.

**Interviewer:** How did you feel about the photos taken of you?

**Participant:** I rarely take photos of myself, but in the last year or two, I put that down to losing my memories and forgetting things — so taking a photo of myself has become more of a recent thing. As for how I felt: I'd say I was glad to be in the present, and quite appreciative that there's a person out there who wants to capture particular moments that are valuable to them.

**Interviewer:** Was your castle visit for a particular occasion, or just a fun date idea?

**Participant:** I've travelled a lot around the world but not really my own country, so this year I made a resolution to explore more of the UK. It was on my list, and that particular weekend I chose a place called Bamburgh, in Northumberland.

**Interviewer:** How do you choose these places to visit and photograph?

**Participant:** There are a couple of things that drive my passion — natural geography, or historical or modern buildings, architecture. So a lot of my photos, if not of myself, are either natural landscapes or modern architecture.

**Interviewer:** Is it because you know the history, or because you want to learn more?

**Participant:** It's more about learning once I'm there. I don't research beforehand — I like to live in that moment when I turn up. So if I turn up to a castle, I'll make it a mini task to figure out what it's about, who lived there, what events happened.

**Interviewer:** You let curiosity drive you to places.

**Participant:** Yeah, I'm a last-minute planner in that sense.

**Interviewer:** Do you have a favourite photo you've taken this year?

**Participant:** This is hard — I have a lot. I rarely use the favourites feature, only if I need to post something to Instagram or a story — so it's really just for convenience, not "favourite photos of my life."

**Interviewer:** How do you access the photos you actually love, then? Do you have a system?

**Participant:** No system — I keep it in memory. I'd rather scroll through the whole camera roll and be surprised, than go straight to a picked favourite. When you scroll, you see the folders and context around it, which builds the full picture of what led up to that favourite photo. It's probably part laziness, part being explorative — connected to my lack of planning.

**Interviewer:** Have you found a photo you'd like to share?

**Participant:** This is a photo I took this year at the Van Gogh Museum in Amsterdam, on New Year's. I'd always wanted to see a Van Gogh painting in person, having grown up seeing them everywhere in pop culture. It's one of my favourite photos this year because it's not just a photo, it's also a painting — a two-in-one. I don't own the painting, but I own the photo of it, and some memory of the labour that went into painting a nice summer field.

**Interviewer:** That's really sweet.

**Participant:** There's a lot of emotion that comes through looking at a painting like that. It pulled up a particular memory — I was once in Japan, sitting in a field outside Tokyo overlooking a small hobbyist airport, just watching planes take off, not in a rush to do anything. That's a core memory of mine — sun shining, just chilling, watching planes. That's the connection to the painting; I do like sunny fields.

**Interviewer:** Do you have a photo of yourself in that sunny field in Japan?

**Participant:** I don't — this was pre-2020, before I decided I needed to document my life more. But I do have other photos of sunny, background fields with similar vibes — this one's from Germany, near Strasbourg, after I flew into Stuttgart and drove across.

**Interviewer:** How many photos do you have in your camera roll?

**Participant:** Around 1,500 last year. I'm almost out of storage, verging on needing a new phone. I do upload everything to iCloud, it's just that the sheer number of photos has carried across every iPhone generation.

**Interviewer:** If somebody scrolled through your camera roll, what impression would they get? What do you think it says about you?

**Participant:** It's a blend of architecture — I'm really into brutalism, specifically the subtler kind, not the ugly extreme concrete buildings — and natural landscapes. If you looked, it'd be a mix of buildings, food, natural landscapes, a few odd selfies, and zoomed photos of old people's faces. Recently it's been filling up with photos of my partner too.

**Interviewer:** If you were a stranger looking at your own camera roll, who would you assume this person is?

**Participant:** I'd say the person is intriguing, blending man-made and nature — combining those into two different artistic styles. There's a beauty in placing an "ugly" piece of architecture, as many people call brutalism, into a nice natural environment. There's a place for everything.

**Interviewer:** What do you usually do with these photos — do you share them anywhere else, besides Instagram?

**Participant:** It depends on the quality and content. Sometimes I'll send an image of something political to a particular group chat, depending on their leanings — current issues, environmental issues, and so on. I also have a film camera I use rarely, when I remember to bring it on holiday — I like the appeal of physical media, being able to keep it in a binder or hang it on a wall.

**Interviewer:** Would you say you care less about composition for the group chats than for what you post publicly on Instagram?

**Participant:** If it's public, I don't put much weight on finding the perfect photo — life isn't perfect, you don't have to frame everything into a perfect shot. A running joke with friends is that my photos are always slightly tilted, because of my bad framing — but to me that reflects the imperfect outside world.

**Interviewer:** Do you edit your photos in any way?

**Participant:** No, I've never edited photos before posting. I take them as they're captured through the lens. I understand why people edit, since your eye and the camera perceive light differently, but for me the answer is no.

**Interviewer:** Can you tell me about a time somebody shared a photo of you without asking? What happened, how did you feel, and did you do anything?

**Participant:** Earlier this year, some friends visited from around the world, and I was showing them around my city, so people were taking photos as we went — awkward photos, candid shots when I wasn't looking. I don't like to gatekeep what my friends post of me — being in public, you're bound to get captured by everything. If my friends photograph me, I give indirect consent, since I'm expecting them to document the visit. It's different with things like CCTV or Meta glasses, where you're never aware you're being recorded — that consent isn't there. But for friends, I'm fine with it.

**Interviewer:** So being in the photo with your friends is a kind of indirect consent to it being posted? Would you rather they check with you first, though?

**Participant:** I do think it's a kind thing to ask before posting, especially now — in the age of AI, your photos posted online go straight into training data for large models, so people should understand their personal images are as valuable as gold in the digital world. Being aware of that and asking for consent is a nice thing to do. If they don't ask, I don't take it to heart, but I might casually mention the world we live in now. I'd like it if they asked, but I'm indifferent when they don't.

**Interviewer:** Would you say privacy is a big concern for you when it comes to the platforms you post on?

**Participant:** Yes, I'm very aware of privacy issues, more of an advocate for privacy — partly from research and publications I've come across on privacy-preserving technology. It's one of my top concerns when it comes to what gets posted about me, or what I produce online. I used to have more control or curation over images of myself.
