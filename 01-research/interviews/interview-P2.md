# Interview P2

**Research project:** Why We Photograph: Meaning and Motivation Behind the Everyday Photo
**Date:** 21 August 2026

---

**Interviewer:** Can you tell me about the last time you had your photo taken?

**Participant:** I was out in a social setting with friends — dinner, because one of my friends was graduating and starting a postdoc. We had a group photo together. I don't usually have my own photo taken; when I do, it's usually within a group photo rather than of me specifically.

**Interviewer:** What's your favourite photo you've taken this year?

**Participant:** Sometime this year I went to Sydney to visit a friend, and I was using an instant camera. One of the fun things about instant cameras — unlike digital — is there's only one copy of the photo, which adds to the memorability of it. I took a picture doing street photography, and when you use an instant camera, it's really hard to get everything right, so when it clicks, it really clicks.

**Interviewer:** Can you tell me more about street photography?

**Participant:** It's taking a picture of a moment. When I look at a scene, there isn't always a story behind everything, but for street photography, that's my interpretation of the story I saw in the scene. If I see someone looking down, even if they're just reading a book, depending on the surroundings there might be something that implies they're feeling something, and I want to capture that.

**Interviewer:** Is it usually people?

**Participant:** Mostly people. Sometimes architecture.

**Interviewer:** When you're out and about, what makes something feel worth photographing?

**Participant:** A lot of the time it's how I feel. The scene either resonates with me, or relates to what I'm feeling throughout the day. When I look at pictures, it's not just what's in the scene, it's my experience with the scene — and that's what I want to capture too.

**Interviewer:** Are you always on the lookout, or do you go out specifically to take photographs?

**Participant:** A couple of months ago I'd have said the latter — going out explicitly to look for photos. But one of my recent pickups is a really small pocket camera, because I kept finding that when I was out without the intent to take photographs, something interesting would happen and I'd wish I'd brought a camera. Now I always have one with me. I also use going out to take pictures as an excuse to go for a walk.

**Interviewer:** As someone who doesn't know much about the technical side — how is a compact camera better than a phone?

**Participant:** The main difference is that a phone photo is really overprocessed — the term is dynamic range. Whatever's bright looks really bright, whatever's dark is really dark, and it's not that the hardware is bad, it's how it's processed. When I take street photographs, if I want something dark, I want it to be black — because that helps isolate the scene. Photography is about directing where people who see your photos look. If something is really dark and not visible, the viewer will automatically look for the bright parts. That's something I play with a lot. With a camera, I can customise the settings more.

**Interviewer:** Can you tell me what you usually do with the photos? Do you share them anywhere?

**Participant:** There's a running joke in photography — when I take a picture of you, you'll never see it. I find the joy of photography more in the taking than in editing. I know people who feel the opposite. For me, I edit whenever I feel like editing, which means I have thousands of pictures in the backlog I haven't touched. But that's like having a personal photo book — when I do feel like editing, I pull it up and go, oh my god, I took these, this one's not that good — but sometimes even bad photos have a good memory attached, and that's worthwhile.

**Interviewer:** If someone scrolled through your camera roll or photo book, what impression would they get?

**Participant:** There are two approaches — take as many pictures as possible and one sticks, or be more intentional. I've been trying to be more intentional, partly because with instant cameras, every picture takes time and has a monetary cost, so I ask myself: do I really want this picture? Sometimes I give it a couple of seconds and decide to move on. Cameras nowadays have screens, but I intentionally block them out — I look with my eye, and if it feels right, I shoot and forget.

**Interviewer:** Can you tell me about a time someone shared a photo of you without asking?

**Participant:** When I'm doing street photography myself, there are other people doing it too, and I may — without knowing — be photographed by them. But I understand, because I'm doing the same activity myself, so it's fine. I've also had experiences where I photographed someone, walked away, and they came up and asked if I'd taken their picture. It's not always bad — sometimes they're just curious, "did I look good, can I see?" People forget that not everything is bad. If they look good and you show them, they might even ask for the photo. It's a good way to make friends, surprisingly, and a good interaction to have with people.

**Interviewer:** Is there anything else you'd like to add?

**Participant:** Are you guys more interested in building something physical, or something more digital?

**Interviewer:** We're definitely interested in something physical, because of the tactility of it.

**Participant:** I look forward to seeing what you do.
